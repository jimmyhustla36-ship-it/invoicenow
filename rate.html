How to work out what you should charge as a freelancer

Most people set a rate by guessing, or by copying whatever a competitor charges. The reliable way is to work backwards from the money you need to keep. Four things eat the gap between your rate and your take-home pay:

  1. Business costs. Software, hardware, insurance, payment fees, a coworking desk. These come out before you're paid a cent.
  2. Taxes. Self-employed people owe income tax plus self-employment tax. Many US freelancers set aside 25–30% of profit; your bracket and state may differ.
  3. Non-billable hours. This is the one that wrecks the maths. Pitching, invoicing, email, admin and marketing are unpaid. A 40-hour week is usually 20–30 billable hours, and a rate calculated on 40 leaves you short.
  4. Time off. No paid vacation, no paid sick days. Four weeks off means you have 48 weeks to earn a full year of income.

The formula this calculator uses

Gross revenue needed = (target take-home ÷ (1 − tax rate)) + business costs. Divide that by your realistic billable hours per year — (52 − weeks off) × billable hours per week — and round up. That's your floor, not your ceiling: value-based pricing can go well above it, but charging below it means working at a loss once the year is totalled up.

Common questions

Why is my calculated rate so much higher than a salaried hourly wage? Because an employee's salary sits on top of employer-paid taxes, benefits, equipment, paid leave and the hours they spend in meetings. Your rate has to cover all of that itself.

How many billable hours are realistic? 25 a week is a healthy planning number for solo freelancers. 30+ is achievable in busy stretches, but assuming it year-round tends to produce a rate that quietly underpays you.

Should I quote hourly or by project? Work out the hourly floor first, then quote projects by estimated hours × that rate. Clients usually prefer a fixed number, and you still know your limit.
