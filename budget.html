How the 50/30/20 budget works

The 50/30/20 rule splits your take-home pay — the amount that actually lands in your account after tax — into three buckets: 50% for needs, 30% for wants, and 20% for savings and debt payoff. It's popular because it's simple enough to actually follow, and because it forces a savings number instead of leaving savings as "whatever's left," which is usually nothing.

What counts as a need vs a want

This is where most people get stuck. A useful test: a need is something that causes a real problem if you stop paying it this month — housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, transport to work, minimum debt payments. A want is everything that makes life better but wouldn't break anything — restaurants, streaming, hobbies, travel, upgrades. Groceries are a need; the same food eaten at a restaurant is a want.

What to do when the numbers don't fit

If your needs are well over 50%, you're not failing at budgeting — you probably live somewhere expensive, which is why the 60/20/20 option exists above. Housing is the line item that decides this, and it's the hardest to change quickly. In the meantime, protect the savings bucket before the wants bucket: it's the one that compounds.

Common questions

  • Should I use gross or take-home pay? Take-home. Budgeting off gross pay builds a plan around money you never receive.
  • Does debt payoff count as savings? Minimum payments are a need. Anything extra you throw at debt belongs in the 20% bucket — paying off a 22% credit card is a better guaranteed return than most investments.
  • What about irregular income? Budget on your lowest typical month, and treat anything above that as savings-bucket money.
